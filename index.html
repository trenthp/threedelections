    How to read the map
    Election
    Pick President or House and a year from the bar under the map, or use ← → for the year and ↑ ↓ for the race. Dimmed years had no race of that kind; clicking one switches race. House results sum every district's votes by county. Midterm turnout is lower than in presidential years, so blocks are shorter and circles smaller.
    Pinned county
    Click or tap a county to pin its details, or find it with the search box (press / to jump there; you can add a state, as in “cook il”). Details stay put while you switch elections, and a chart shows the county's margin in every election: solid dots for presidential races, hollow dots for House races (which aren't directly comparable). Click a dot to jump to that election. Click the county again, press Esc or use × to unpin. Copy link shares exactly what's on screen.
    Height
    Flat 2D is a standard map. Votes raises each county by votes cast per square mile. Net margin raises it by the winner's vote lead per square mile, so close races stay low even in big cities.
    Scale
    How vote density turns into height. Linear is the most literal: block volume is proportional to votes, so a few big cities tower over everything. Square root tames the tallest counties so mid-sized ones are visible too. Log spreads out the smallest counties.
    Exaggeration
    Stretches every block's height by the same factor. It makes short counties easier to see without changing how they compare.
    Color
    Margin colors every county by how lopsided its result was, so large, sparse counties take up a lot of the map. Weighted fades sparsely populated counties toward gray, so color strength follows where people vote. Radial draws a circle in each county whose area is proportional to its votes, so the amount of color matches the amount of voters. Winner uses a solid party color, however close the race.
    View
    Camera presets. Drag to rotate, right-drag (or two-finger drag) to pan, and scroll or pinch to zoom. Reset view appears once you've moved the camera. Press Space to switch between flat and 3D. On a phone, drag the bar at the top of the panel to make it smaller or larger.

    Height and color can both show how many people voted. For the most balanced picture, pair Votes height with Radial or Weighted color. For the familiar election-night map, use Flat 2D with Margin. The Land area and Votes bars above compare the two directly.

    About the data
    • 2020 and 2024 presidential: tonmcg/US_County_Level_Election_Results. Boundaries: U.S. Census Bureau via us-atlas. Land area is computed from each county's real geographic area, not its on-screen size.
    • 2004–2016 presidential and 2004–2026 House: counts from The New York Times and the Associated Press, compiled by mike10010100/elections-2026. House results sum every district's votes by county.
    • 2026 counts are unofficial. Some counties are still counting.
    • Gaps: Hawaii presidential results for 2008–2016 and DC's for 2008 come from tonmcg's 2008–2016 file (Hawaii is within about 1% of certified totals). There are no Hawaii results for 2004, none for DC in 2004, 2012 or 2016, and no Hawaii House results before 2024. DC has no voting House seat.
    • Uncontested House seats show one-sided margins and are faded in the county chart. Some states leave unopposed races off the ballot entirely (for example Florida, Oklahoma, Arkansas and Louisiana), so those counties have no House result that year.
    • Alaska reports results by state house district, which don't line up with boroughs, so Alaska is shown as one uniform statewide result. Its 2022 House count is the first round of ranked-choice voting.
    • Connecticut 2024 results are reported by the new planning regions. They're mapped onto the legacy counties with an approximate crosswalk.
    • Alaska and Hawaii are drawn as insets at a different scale, so their volumes aren't comparable with the lower 48.
    • This is an independent visualization. It isn't affiliated with any campaign, party, news organization or election office.
